## Key Idea
TOR hidden services conceal the server's IP address, but the server has to stay online and answer anyone who connects, and such constant exposure is what most deanonymization attacks (e.g., guard discovery) exploit. **VaulTor** lets the content provider hand its service off to a volunteer "vault" that runs it inside a trusted execution environment (Intel SGX), so the vault serves clients without being able to read or alter the content. The provider then only connects now and then to push updates, and no one can reach it on demand.

## Critique
The core argument is convincing: most of the attacks the paper lists rely on the provider being always online and reachable on demand. VaulTor takes that away without changing the client's path, so the only cost is TEE overhead, which is still neglible compared to the 6-hop onion routing overhead. 
But I feel like the paper doesn't fundamentally resolve the risk, because the vault just becomes another long-running hidden service exposed to the same attacks. While the server may stay anonoymous (and arguably safer), its content can still be taken down.
In addition, the security evaluations are mostly qualitiative, and the paper's experiments only measure latency. 

## Connections
Like [OpenFlame](https://danielja.ng/cs440-blog/2026/11/04/dns/), VaulTor is a federated design that only works if independent operators choose to run part of the infrastructure. Both papers treat the question of why anyone would do that as an afterthought: OpenFLAME never explains why organizations would publish their maps, and VaulTor offers crypto payments or altruism to vault operators who take on real legal risk.
