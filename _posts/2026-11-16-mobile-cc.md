## Key Idea
On cellular networks, the radio link's capacity changes faster than end-to-end congestion control can estimate it from ACKs and RTTs, so senders either leave capacity unused or build long queues at the cellular link. The paper's main insight is that the phone doesn't need to estimate this at all, because its modem already receives the base station's bandwidth grants every few milliseconds. The authors build **OpenDiag**, a kernel module that exposes these radio statistics in real time on unrooted Android phones, and **BISCAY**, which sets the congestion window directly from the granted bandwidth, typically cutting delay by more than 90% without losing throughput.

## Critique
The most convincing result is the sampling-rate experiment: once BISCAY reads radio statistics less often than every ~100 ms, its advantage disappears, which shows that the gains come from fresh measurements and that OpenDiag is a necessary contribution. 
I'm less convinced by the emulation results, since in Pantheon BISCAY replays bandwidth computed from KPIs recorded alongside the very same trace that drives the emulated link, so it gets near-perfect knowledge of the link (correlation above 0.95 as reported earlier in the paper), while every other CC algorithms have to estimate it. 
Those KPIs were also recorded while UDP traffic saturated the link, which points to how BISCAY may affect its own signal: the base station's scheduler decides grants partly from how much data the phone reports as waiting, so if BISCAY keeps the outgoing buffer nearly empty, it may receive smaller grants than the ones it replays, and the paper never examines this.


## Connections
Like [LeoCC](/cs440-blog/2026/11/09/leo/) and [Vidaptive](/cs440-blog/2026/11/11/realtime-video/), BISCAY targets a link that changes faster than a general-purpose congestion controller can observe, and like [dcSim's](/cs440-blog/2026/10/21/datacenter-transport/) probes and [DCP's](/cs440-blog/2026/10/26/rdma/) trimmed headers, it improves performance by replacing the congestion signal with a better one. Interestingly, instead of inferring the network's state from the endpoint (like LeoCC), BISCAY reads it directly from the hardware that allocates the bandwidth, which suggests that the best signal may actually exist below the transport layer.
