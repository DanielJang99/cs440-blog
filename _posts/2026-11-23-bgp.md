## Key Idea
In a BGP hijack attack, an adversary reroutes traffic for a victim's prefix through its own network before delivering it, and keeps its fake announcements hidden from the route monitors that control-plane defenses rely on. The paper's insight is that when the detour passes through another country, the extra distance adds significant propagation delay. The authors build **HiDe**, which runs on a programmable switch at the network edge, passively tracks the minimum RTT per destination prefix, and flags an attack when that minimum jumps above the lowest delay physically possible through a threat region. 


## Critique
The most convincing part is moving hijack detection into the data plane: existing defenses mostly watch BGP announcements, which a stealthy attacker can keep away from route monitors, whereas the extra propagation delay of a long detour shows up in the victim's own traffic and can't be hidden. 
However, a higher delay floor doesn't necessarily mean a hijack: the per-window minimum RTT filters out transient congestion, but a benign reroute (e.g., after a cable cut) or a downstream queue that stays full longer than a window looks just like an attack, and since HiDe can't tell the causes apart, it would block a legitimate prefix until the delay drops. 
I also find the threat model quite niche, as the adversaries that pose the biggest national security threats are often neighbors (e.g., South Korea vs North Korea, Israel vs Palestine), which are exactly the short detours the authors admit RTT can't detect.
