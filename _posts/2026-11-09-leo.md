## Key Idea
Fast-moving LEO satellites cause sudden capacity changes, RTT jumps, and random loss that existing congestion control algorithms mistake for congestion or fail to track. 
The paper's main insight is that such variation happens at periodic "reconfigurations," when Starlink re-plans each dish's satellite link. **LeoCC** detects these from the endpoint via ligthweight pings. Its two-state rate controller then uses full bandwidth while a path is stable, and drains the queue and re-probes the new path after each reconfiguration.


## Critique
The methodology and evaluation is convincing: the authors found the problem by measuring live Starlink terminals, then evaluated LeoCC both in the wild and on a controlled testbed that replays recorded Starlink traces.
I'm less convinced about the claim that LeoCC deploys easily. For downloads (which are most of Starlink traffic), the sender is the remote server, so the content provider must run LeoCC and read the reconfiguration hints the user’s device sends back in its ACKs. The paper’s downlink experiments used servers the authors controlled, so they didn’t test whether that works in practice.


## Connections
Much like papers on datacenter transports (DCTCP, [dcSim](/cs440-blog/2026/10/21/datacenter-transport/)), LeoCC shows that general-purpose congestion control breaks when its assumptions about the network don’t hold, and that a design tailored to the network's empirical behavior works better. 
In addition, similar to DCTCP’s ECN marks, [dcSim’s probes](/cs440-blog/2026/10/21/datacenter-transport/) and [DCP’s trimmed headers](/cs440-blog/2026/10/26/rdma/), LeoCC replaces loss as the congestion signal with a better one (the timing of reconfigurations), suggesting that transport protocol is only as good as its signal about the network.
