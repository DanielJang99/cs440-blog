## Key Idea
How hypergiants configure and scale their QUIC deployments is largely hidden, and active scanning to find out is regarded intrusive. The authors instead mine QUIC backscatter at the UCSD network telescope, i.e., server handshake replies to Initials spoofed with telescope addresses, and show that certain cleartext header fields from QUIC (connection IDs, packet lengths, retransmission timing) are enough to fingerprint each operator's stack. Their core contribution is exploiting the structured server connection IDs that QUIC-aware load balancers rely on, which lets them detect off-net servers passively. 


## Critique
The Meta case is the most convincing part: since mvfst's CID encoding is open source, the authors have real ground truth, and they caught Meta's 10-day migration from SCIDv1 to SCIDv2 purely from backscatter before confirming it with active probes. Less convincing is how far the headline result generalizes, since counting L7LBs only works for Meta (the one operator with a documented cleartext encoding), and interpreting host IDs after the v2 migration, when the same IDs repeat across clusters, depends on cluster structure learned from active scans and reverse DNS. 

## Connections
In my [P-ECMP post](/cs440-blog/2026/10/07/multi-path/) I noted that lack of observability is a recurring theme in our readings. This paper is the inverse case, where infrastructure that operators deliberately hide behind VIPs and encryption becomes visible anyway through (quite incidentally) signals like connection IDs and retransmission timing. In addition, like [Misa et al.'s](/cs440-blog/2026/09/28/internet-invariants/), this paper works backwards from observed behavior to the design goals behind it.
